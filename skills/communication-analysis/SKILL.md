# Communication Analysis

This skill applies Friedemann Schulz von Thun's four-sides ("four ears") model
and Carl Rogers-style active listening to analyze or improve interpersonal
communication: a message, a piece of feedback, a conversation transcript, or a
draft reply. The goal is to decode what is actually being communicated on
several levels at once, notice where signals contradict each other, and
suggest concrete, non-manipulative ways to make communication clearer.

## When to use

Whenever reviewing or rewriting feedback, analyzing a conversation for what is
really being communicated, diagnosing why a message landed badly, or
discussing how to set and respect interpersonal boundaries. Also covers the
four comprehensibility makers, the relevance filter, why disturbances take
precedence, and the solution-reflex trap — plus mirroring hidden appeals
instead of reacting to them blindly.

## Scope and disclaimer

This is educational material about interpersonal communication theory, not
medical, psychological, or therapeutic advice. It does not diagnose people or
relationships, and it is not a substitute for therapy or professional
counseling. Apply it when explicitly asked to analyze, review, or rewrite
communication — do not use it to unilaterally psychoanalyze a third party from
a message they did not intend for analysis. Treat every framework here as a
lens to offer, not a verdict to impose; people vary, and any single
interpretation of a message may be wrong.

## The four-sides model

Every message can be decoded on four levels simultaneously (Schulz von Thun,
*Miteinander Reden Band 1*). When analyzing a message, read it from all four
sides before reacting to just one.

| Side | Question it answers | Example decoding of "It's dark in here" |
|------|---------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Factual** (Sachebene) | What is being stated? | The room's lighting level is low. |
| **Self-revealing** (Selbstkundgabe) | What does this reveal about the sender? | The sender is uncomfortable or wants something. |
| **Relationship** (Beziehungsebene) | What does this say about how the sender sees the relationship? | The sender feels entitled to comment / expects the listener to act. |
| **Appeal** (Appellebene) | What does the sender want the listener to do? | "Turn on the light" (often left unstated — see Hidden appeals below). |

When analyzing a message or piece of feedback, name what is being communicated
on each of the four sides explicitly, rather than only responding to the
factual content. Miscommunication is frequently a mismatch between the side
the sender intended and the side the receiver decoded — for example, a
manager's factual status update ("I'm prioritizing something else today")
landing on an employee's relationship ear as "my work doesn't matter." When
reviewing a conflict or a conversation that went badly, checking whether both
sides were tuned to the same "ear" is often more revealing than checking who
was factually right.

## Congruence and incongruence

Congruence is Carl Rogers's term for alignment between what is said and how it is said, one of his core conditions alongside empathy and unconditional positive regard. **Incongruence** is when verbal and nonverbal (or written and contextual)
signals contradict each other — e.g., "I'm fine" said with a visibly upset
expression, or a message whose words are calm but whose punctuation, timing,
or channel signal urgency or distress. Incongruent messages put the receiver
in a bind: they don't know which signal to trust, which blocks effective
communication.

When reviewing a message or transcript, check for incongruence:

1. Do the stated content and the tone/framing/nonverbal cues point the same
   direction?
2. If not, name the contradiction directly rather than picking one signal to
   respond to. Example confrontation: "You're saying you're fine, but
   something in how you said it suggests otherwise — what's actually going
   on?"
3. When drafting or rewriting a message, prefer **authentic self-revelation
    without over-exposure** over incongruent statements, following Ruth C. Cohn's
    TZI helper rule to be authentic and selective. The guiding rule:
    *everything I say is true, but not everything true has to be said.* Instead
   of "I'm fine" (while visibly not fine), a congruent alternative is "I'm not
   doing great, but I don't want to get into it right now" — honest, and it
   still lets the sender keep a boundary around what they disclose.

Congruent communication is not the same as full disclosure. It means: what you
do say should be true and should not contradict how you say it. Selective
disclosure based on context and relevance is legitimate; sending mixed signals
is not.

## Optimizing the factual level

Two guiding rules and four quality dimensions apply
specifically to keeping the factual content of a conversation effective: the relevance filter
from Schulz von Thun, the disturbance postulate from Ruth C. Cohn's TZI, and the four
Hamburg comprehensibility makers from Langer, Schulz von Thun, and Tausch.

### "Does this belong here?" — the relevance filter

Before adding a point to a conversation, check: is this relevant to the
current topic, and does it help clarify or resolve it — or is it a
distraction? Applying this filter keeps conversations focused, saves time and
cognitive resources, and prevents drifting into unrelated detail. Use it both
when reviewing a message ("is every sentence here doing work?") and when
drafting one.

### "Disturbances take precedence"

This is Ruth C. Cohn's TZI postulate "Störungen haben Vorrang" — disturbances take precedence in the
sense that they take it whether invited or not, not as a procedure to follow. Unresolved friction,
confusion, or emotional disturbance in a conversation should be addressed rather than parked until
the end, but whether to interrupt the main thread now or to note the disturbance and return to it
is a judgment call for the situation.

Waiting until the end to raise accumulated objections tends to produce a pile
of unresolved friction and a more emotionally loaded conversation; addressing
each disturbance as it appears keeps the conversation clearer and less costly
for both sides. When reviewing a transcript, flag places where a disturbance
was visible but got deferred or ignored — that is usually where the
conversation degrades.

### The four comprehensibility makers

Use these four dimensions together — not in isolation — when reviewing or
drafting a message for clarity:

| Dimension | What it means | Optimum |
|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Simplicity** | State complex things in an understandable way; avoid unnecessary complexity. | More is better, optimum at the maximum. |
| **Structure** | Logically ordered, clearly sequenced information with visible relationships between parts. | More is better, optimum at the maximum. |
| **Brevity / conciseness** | Get to the point without unnecessary digression, while keeping the relevant information complete. | Middle optimum: too short and too long both harm understanding. |
| **Stimulance** | Keep it engaging enough to hold the receiver's attention. | Least important; sparing use only, extra stimulance can harm when it costs brevity or covers weak structure. |

Simplicity and structure carry the most weight. When reviewing a message against these four, judge the balance with that weighting in mind — the fix for one dimension can quietly break another (e.g.
tightening for brevity can remove structure).

## The appeal level: hidden appeals and mirroring

A **hidden appeal** (verdeckter Appell) is when a sender wants something but
does not ask for it directly, instead wrapping the request in a seemingly
neutral statement (e.g., "This work is really difficult..." meaning "please
help me"). People reach for hidden appeals out of fear of direct rejection,
social norms against asking directly, a wish to keep the responsibility on the
receiver's side, or a wish to avoid vulnerability.

Hidden appeals carry real risk: they can shade into manipulation, they invite
misreading, they erode trust if the receiver later feels manipulated, and they
are simply less efficient than asking directly.

**Mirroring** (Spiegelung) is the resolution technique:

1. Identify the likely hidden appeal.
2. Put the suspected intent into words explicitly.
3. Pair the reflection with an open question, e.g.: "I hear that you'd like me
   to help with this — is that right?"

This puts the ball back in the sender's court: they can now confirm the
intent openly, or correct the interpretation, but either way they are pushed
toward transparent communication. Mirroring is generally more honest and more
direct than either guessing silently or ignoring the hidden appeal, and it
builds trust through transparency rather than assuming manipulative intent.

Use mirroring when reviewing or drafting a *reply* to a message that seems to
carry an unstated request. Use it as an offered clarification, not an
accusation — the tone should invite correction, not assert that manipulation
occurred.

## Active listening

Active listening was named by Carl Rogers and Richard Farson: listening that brings about change
because the speaker feels fully understood and accepted. Rogers's core conditions for that effect
are empathy, genuineness (congruence), and unconditional positive regard. In practice it calls for
three things at once:

1. **Full attention** — deliberately setting aside mental background
   processes (to-do lists, pre-formed replies, other distractions), similar in
   spirit to "clearing your head" before focusing. In face-to-face settings
   this shows up as physical orientation toward the speaker and eliminating
   external distractions.
2. **Active understanding** — simultaneously tracking multiple layers: the
   literal content, the emotional subtext, nonverbal signals (where visible),
   and unspoken needs behind the words. This is understanding *before*
   interpretation, not instead of it.
3. **Constructive feedback** — reflecting what was heard back to the sender:
   - **Paraphrasing**: restating what was heard in your own words to validate
     understanding.
   - **Emotional mirroring**: acknowledging and reflecting back the feeling
     dimension, not only the content.
   - **Targeted follow-up questions**: open questions to go deeper, closed
     questions to pin down specifics.

Rogers's premise was that people open up and develop only when they feel fully
understood and accepted — feeling understood is not a "nice extra" but a
precondition for someone being willing to reconsider a position or change
behavior. When analyzing why a conversation stalled or a piece of advice
landed badly, check whether the receiver felt understood *before* anything
resembling a solution was offered.

### The solution-reflex warning

The **solution reflex** is the near-automatic urge to give advice or fix a
problem the moment someone describes a challenge. It short-circuits
understanding: attention shifts to formulating a response, context gets
missed, and the emotional or unspoken dimension of what's being said gets
lost.

Premature solutions tend to backfire because:

- They are based on a fragment of the picture, not the full one.
- The surface-level problem is often not the real concern.
- Sometimes the person needs a sounding board, not an answer, and jumping to
  a fix removes their chance to work it out themselves.
- Fast solutions can implicitly signal "I want this conversation to be over,"
  not "I understand you."

Countermeasures, in order:

1. **Pause deliberately** between hearing and responding.
2. **Understand before solving** — fully grasp what's meant before reacting.
3. **Ask, don't answer** — "tell me more" rather than "have you tried...".
4. **Ask permission** before switching into advice mode: "Do you want
   suggestions, or do you just want me to listen right now?"

The solution reflex is the direct opposite of active listening's third
dimension (constructive feedback): paraphrasing and emotional mirroring keep a
conversation in understanding mode instead of letting it jump to solving mode.

## Workflow: applying this to a concrete task

Use this checklist whichever task triggered the analysis — reviewing
feedback, rewriting a message, or analyzing a conversation transcript.

1. **Read the message on all four sides.** Name factual, self-revealing,
   relationship, and appeal content explicitly, even where the appeal is
   implicit.
2. **Check congruence.** Do stated content and tone/framing point the same
   way? If not, name the contradiction rather than silently picking a side.
3. **Check relevance and disturbances.** Does every part of the message
   belong, and was any visible friction addressed when it appeared or left to
   fester?
4. **Check the four comprehensibility makers.** Simplicity and structure: more is better.
   Brevity: middle optimum. Stimulance: least important, extra can harm.
5. **Look for a hidden appeal.** If the message seems to want something
   without asking, offer a mirrored reflection with an open question rather
   than reacting to (or ignoring) the surface content.
6. **Check for the solution reflex.** Before proposing fixes, confirm the
   core concern has actually been understood — paraphrase it back if unsure.
7. **When rewriting:** produce a version that is congruent (says one thing,
   consistently), relevant (says only what belongs), appropriately
   comprehensible (simple, structured, concise, engaging), and — if there was
   a hidden appeal — makes the request explicit instead of indirect.

## Boundaries and congruent communication

Congruent, honest communication is closely tied to how clearly people draw
and respect boundaries with each other. This closing section generalizes
boundary-setting ideas from Jesper Juul's family work on parent-child
relationships to partnership, work, and friendship.

- **The freedom principle.** Personal freedom ends where it endangers someone
  else's freedom. This is a general boundary principle across all relationship
  types, not specific to any one of them. It requires active reflection, not
  passive assumption: where does my freedom end, where does the other
  person's begin, and how do I communicate that boundary?
- **Closeness through boundaries, not despite them.** Genuinely close
  relationships are not built on boundarylessness but on clear boundaries.
  Boundaries protect against enmeshment and loss of identity, allow a real
  encounter between two distinct people, and prevent unhealthy dependency.
  Without boundaries, relationships tend toward diffuse roles, emotional
  overload, and loss of identity rather than closer connection.
- **Boundaries are bidirectional.** Respecting boundaries is not only about
  setting your own; it equally means recognizing and respecting the other
  person's boundaries — their autonomy, their emotional and physical limits,
  their right not to disclose everything. Bidirectional boundary-respect
  applies across contexts: clarifying expectations and individual space in a
  partnership, maintaining professional distance and limiting availability at
  work, and being explicit about reciprocity and capacity in friendships.
- **Boundaries want proactive communication, not constant renegotiation.**
  Stating expectations or constraints up front (e.g., naming in advance that a
  block of time needs to be uninterrupted) tends to prevent the conflicts that
  arise from mismatched, unstated expectations — rather than relying on
  boundaries being renegotiated in the moment, every time.

Read together with the sections above: a congruent message is honest without
over-exposing, an appropriately bounded message says only what's relevant, and
a well-communicated boundary is itself an act of clear, direct communication
rather than a wall against it.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Reacting to only one of the four sides.** Responding only to the factual
  content of a message that was really a relationship-level complaint (or
  vice versa) is a frequent source of talking past each other.
- **Treating incongruence as something to route around.** Ignoring the
  contradiction and just picking whichever signal is more convenient leaves
  the receiver without real clarity.
- **Over-indexing on one comprehensibility dimension.** Maximizing brevity or
  stimulance at the expense of the others degrades the message rather than
  improving it.
- **Diagnosing manipulation from a hidden appeal.** Mirroring should be
  offered as an open, correctable reflection — asserting that someone is
  manipulating rather than asking "is that what you meant?" turns a
  clarifying technique into an accusation.
- **Jumping to solutions.** The solution reflex is the most common way a
  well-intentioned response undercuts active listening; check for it before
  offering fixes.
- **Treating this as a diagnostic tool for people, not messages.** The
  frameworks describe patterns in communication, not fixed traits of a
  person; apply them to the message or exchange under review, not as a
  verdict on someone's character.
