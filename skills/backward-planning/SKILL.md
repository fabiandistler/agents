# Backward Planning Against Procrastination

Use this when a task or project has stalled and the person cannot say why they
haven't started. The point is not to produce a better plan — plans that stay
abstract don't get acted on. The point is to turn the goal into a concrete next
step small enough to actually do, and to expose whatever is actually stopping
the person, so the plan and the excuse can be dealt with separately instead of
tangled together.

**Origin note**: this method is drawn from Barbara Sher & Bettina Lemke's book
on "Scanner" personalities (*Du musst dich nicht entscheiden, wenn du tausend
Träume hast*), which observes that multi-interested people rarely stall out of
laziness — they stall because a project feels too big, perfectionism blocks
the first move, committing feels risky, or the "right" conditions never
arrive. The method below is general-purpose and not specific to any
personality type; treat it as one useful lens, not a diagnosis.

## Step 0: Is this procrastination at all?

Before applying the method, ask directly — don't assume. Stalling has two very
different causes and they need different responses:

| Signal | Genuine procrastination (avoidance) | Rational deferral |
|---|---|---|
| Why hasn't this started? | Vague, or an emotional reason surfaces (fear, "not the right time," "not good enough yet") | A clear, specific competing priority or missing resource |
| How do they feel when the topic comes up? | Guilt, anxiety, deflection | Neutral — "yes, deliberately not now" |
| Does the reason change if you ask "what would happen if you started today anyway?" | Reveals a feared outcome (criticism, failure, exposure) | Reason holds up: still blocked by the same external constraint |
| Is there an actual competing commitment? | No, or a vague one used as cover | Yes, named and real |

Ask these questions out loud rather than guessing. If the answers land clearly
in the "rational deferral" column, the honest output is to consciously
reschedule or drop the task — do not run the rest of this method on something
that is correctly waiting its turn. If the answers land in the "genuine
procrastination" column, or the person can't tell, continue.

## The three steps

### 1. Plan backwards

Start from the goal and work backwards to today, writing down the steps in
reverse order. This does not replace taking action, but it turns an abstract
goal into a sequence of concrete, doable steps — including whatever the very
next physical action would be.

**Use**: an abstract goal ("write the report") becomes a concrete action
("open the doc and write one paragraph on X").

### 2. Surface the hidden fear

Working backwards usually exposes a blocker that a forward-looking plan
hides. Watch for:

- Fear of criticism
- Fear of failure
- A feeling of not being entitled to attempt this
- Unrealistic expectations of what "done" should look like

Don't just note that a blocker exists — name it. Ask directly what the person
is actually afraid will happen, and what would make that outcome survivable or
less likely. The goal is to reduce the specific danger, not to argue the
person out of the feeling.

### 3. Set a real deadline

Agree on a concrete deadline with another person. Social commitment creates
external structure that a private intention doesn't. A deadline that exists
only in the person's own head is not yet a real deadline for this method —
it needs another person attached to it (to report to, to hand something to,
to meet with).

## Supporting techniques

- **Short sprints**: use a 15-minute timer on difficult projects. The timer's
  job is to make starting easy, not to bound the whole task — most people keep
  going once started.
- **Reward**: for dull, necessary tasks, do the duty first, then reward
  yourself with time on an exciting project.
- **Bring in help**: name a friend or colleague as an accountability partner —
  this is also how step 3's deadline gets its "other person."

## Workflow

1. Ask the Step 0 questions. If it's a rational deferral, stop here and help
   reschedule or explicitly drop the task instead — do not force the rest of
   the method onto it.
2. If it's genuine procrastination (or unclear), plan backwards from the goal
   until you reach a next action small enough to do in the next 15 minutes.
3. While planning backwards, ask directly what feeling or fear shows up at
   each point of resistance. Name it; don't paraphrase it away.
4. Ask for one concrete danger-reduction move for that fear (e.g., share a
   draft with one trusted person instead of "everyone," lower the definition
   of "done" for a first pass, separate "starting" from "finishing well").
5. Agree on a real deadline with a named other person — not a date the person
   sets alone.
6. Offer the 15-minute sprint as the way to actually start, right now if
   possible.

## Output

Every run of this method should end with two things stated explicitly:

- **Surfaced blocker(s)**: the specific fear or avoidance reason found in
  step 2, named plainly (not "procrastination" in general).
- **Concrete first step**: one action small enough to fit a 15-minute sprint,
  described specifically enough that there's no ambiguity about what "doing
  it" means.

If step 0 concluded this was a rational deferral, the output is instead an
explicit decision to reschedule or drop the task — that is a legitimate and
sufficient outcome; don't manufacture a fear where there isn't one.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating every stalled task as procrastination. Some deferrals are correct
  triage — check Step 0 first.
- Producing a plan while skipping step 2. A backwards plan without a named
  fear usually just restates the same abstract goal in more steps.
- Accepting a deadline the person sets for themselves alone as satisfying
  step 3. It needs another person attached.
- Treating the 15-minute sprint as the whole solution. It's a way to start,
  not a substitute for finding the blocker.
- Being prescriptive about what the fear "really" is. Ask; don't diagnose.
