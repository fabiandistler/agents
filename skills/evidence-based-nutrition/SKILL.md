# Evidence-Based Nutrition for Body Composition

This skill summarizes evidence-based nutrition principles for body
composition — building muscle and losing fat — covering how to periodize
calories across a training year, how much protein actually helps, a flexible
system for deciding how precisely to track, and where nutrition sits relative
to sleep and training in the list of things that actually move the needle.
It is aimed at people already training with a goal of muscle gain or fat
loss, not at general dietary guidance.

> **Scope and safety note.** This is an educational summary of nutrition
> research and popular evidence-based coaching literature, not medical,
> dietetic, or personalized nutrition advice. Individual needs vary
> (metabolism, medical history, medications, disordered-eating risk,
> pregnancy/lactation, and other conditions can all change what is
> appropriate). Some figures below are explicitly simplifications of a more
> complex reality — this is flagged inline where it applies. Apply this
> material only when explicitly asked to help with body-composition
> nutrition, and treat every number as a starting range to individualize,
> not a prescription. When in doubt, or where a medical condition is
> involved, defer to a qualified professional (physician, registered
> dietitian).

## 1. Calorie periodisation across a training year

Rather than picking one calorie target and holding it indefinitely, a
strategic split of time between surplus, maintenance, and deficit phases is
suggested to maximize muscle gain while limiting fat gain:

| Phase | Share of the year | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Surplus | 50–60% | Muscle gain; ~200–400 kcal above maintenance |
| Maintenance | 20–25% | Plateau management and recovery |
| Deficit | 20–25% | Fat-loss phases |

**Why a small surplus over a large one:** a commonly cited finding (Helms et
al.) is that small surpluses (200–400 kcal) outperform large ones (>500 kcal)
because fat gain rises roughly exponentially with surplus size while the
rate of muscle gain does not scale up proportionally — a large surplus buys
little extra muscle for a lot of extra fat.

**Practical reference points cited alongside this framework:**
- Weight gain during a surplus: roughly 0.25–0.5% body weight per week.
- A bulk-to-cut ratio around 3:1 (e.g. ~12 weeks gaining, ~4 weeks cutting).
- Starting a surplus phase around 10–12% body fat, and cutting again once
  body fat reaches roughly 15–16%, to stay inside a body-fat range (roughly
  10–15%) associated with a favorable muscle-to-fat partitioning.

**Mini-cuts:** short, more aggressive cuts (2–6 weeks at a 500–750 kcal
deficit) can be embedded inside a longer gaining phase. The stated rationale
is that keeping body fat inside the optimal range this way can extend the
total productive muscle-building period by roughly 4–6 additional months,
rather than needing one long, uninterrupted cut later.

**On timeframes generally:** diets reliably take longer than planned. A
frequently cited heuristic ("rule of 2") is that the actual duration of a
diet tends to be about twice the initially planned duration — worth building
into any plan up front rather than treating a missed deadline as failure.

## 2. Protein: 1.6–2.2 g/kg as an evidence-based ceiling, not a floor to exceed

Current meta-analyses point to a fairly narrow range for protein's
hypertrophy benefit:

| Intake | Effect |
|---|---|
| Below 1.6 g/kg | Muscle protein synthesis likely suboptimal |
| 1.6–2.2 g/kg | Range associated with maximal measurable hypertrophy benefit |
| Above 2.2 g/kg | Not shown to add further hypertrophy benefit (also not shown to be harmful) |

The practical implication drawn from this: once intake is comfortably inside
1.6–2.2 g/kg, additional protein is optional rather than beneficial for
muscle gain specifically — remaining calorie budget may be better allocated
to carbohydrate or fat rather than pushed further into protein. For an 80 kg
person this range is roughly 128–176 g/day.

**Meal distribution:** the literature summarized here suggests that a
smaller number of larger, higher-protein meals (3–4/day) performs at least
as well as many small ones (6–8 meals at ~20 g protein each) — the
"anabolic window" for protein synthesis around a meal or a workout appears
considerably longer than once assumed, easing the need for tightly timed
small feedings.

## 3. Other macronutrients (brief)

These figures are supporting context around the protein and calorie targets
above, not independently emphasized:

- **Carbohydrate:** a minimum around 150 g/day is suggested for training
  performance and recovery; carbohydrate available *before* a workout is
  emphasized more than precise post-workout timing.
- **Fat:** roughly 20–35% of total calories, with at least ~0.5 g/kg to
  support hormone production.

## 4. Meal timing and circadian rhythm

Timing nutrient intake in step with the body's circadian rhythm is
associated with better outcomes for muscle gain, fat loss, and recovery:

| Element | Suggested range | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Eating window | 8–12h, sweet spot ~10–12h | Under 8h may limit muscle growth; over 12h works against circadian rhythms |
| First meal | 1–3h after waking | Synchronizes peripheral circadian rhythm; timing matters more than the exact clock time |
| Pre-workout carbs | Emphasized over post-workout | Anabolic window is wider than once thought; total daily carb intake matters more than exact timing |
| Evening cutoff | 1–2h before sleep | Digestion, especially of large or fatty meals, can impair sleep quality |
| Fasting window (if intermittent fasting) | Up to 16–18h | Beyond this, muscle growth appears increasingly limited — a trade-off against autophagy benefits |

## 5. The three-tier tracking system

A situational system for choosing tracking precision, matched to training
phase and life circumstances rather than applied uniformly at all times:

| Tier | Precision | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Best** | Macros to within ±5–10 g | Contest prep — highest precision, used temporarily for a specific goal |
| **Better** | Protein + total calories (±10 g protein, ±100 kcal) | Recovery phases, diet breaks — balances control and flexibility |
| **Good** | Total calories only (±100 kcal) | Maintenance — minimal tracking, sustainable long-term |

Movement between tiers is driven by training phase (contest vs. building vs.
maintaining), life circumstances (travel, social events), and psychological
load from tracking itself — dropping a tier under stress is a legitimate
adjustment, not a failure.

**Alcohol rule:** alcohol counts toward none of the tracked macros and is
capped at roughly 15% of daily calories when it is consumed; a stated
frequency guideline is about 1–2 occasions/week in moderation (less often
during contest prep). Because it displaces macro-specific calories, drinking
typically nudges someone from "Best" toward "Better" or "Good" tier for that
day.

## 6. From tracking to intuitive eating

Durable nutrition competence is described as a gradual transition from
quantitative tracking to qualitative body-signal awareness, without
discarding what tracking taught:

1. **Quantitative phase** — precise tracking builds a reference library:
   portion sizes, macro content of common foods.
2. **Combined phase** — established habits carry over; hunger/satiety
   signals increasingly guide choices while quantitative knowledge sits in
   the background.
3. **Intuitive phase** — body signals are the primary guide; tracking
   knowledge remains available as a safety net when needed.

This transition is described as depending on hunger/satiety signals that
function normally (these can take time to normalize after an extended
contest-prep-style deficit), prior tracking experience to build the
reference knowledge in the first place, and a gradual rather than abrupt
handoff.

## 7. Applying nutrition without precise tracking

Two practical, habit-based approaches for pursuing a body-composition goal
without ongoing macro tracking, intended for someone who already has the
quantitative reference knowledge from phase 1 above:

**Qualitative weight gain (bulking without tracking):**
- To slow gain: increase satiety (feel "a bit fuller" after meals, include
  fiber/produce, avoid highly liquid/blended meals), or shift protein down
  toward ~0.7 g/lb to make room for carbs/fat.
- To correct too-fast gain: increase satiety further (smaller visual
  portions, eat slower, more water/vegetables for volume without calories),
  or raise protein toward ~1.5 g/lb.
- Weigh in minimally (roughly 1 weekday + 1 weekend day) and judge 3-week
  trends rather than daily fluctuations; at maintenance, weighing may be
  skipped entirely in favor of visual assessment.

**Habit-based fat loss (without apps or scales):** systematic weight loss
through specific habits rather than counting — e.g. a consistent, mostly
home-prepared meal structure; protein and produce at every meal; slow eating
with full attention (no phone); minimizing eating out; artificial sweeteners
over sugar; keeping trigger foods out of the house; and stopping "a bit
short of full" rather than finishing what's in front of you. The suggested
rollout is 3–4 such habits at a time for 1–2 months, adding more only if
results stall, and judging by how the person looks rather than the scale.

## 8. Lifestyle priority hierarchy

Nutrition and training decisions sit inside a broader hierarchy of factors
that determine body-composition outcomes. One source in this material
offers an illustrative (and explicitly simplified) breakdown of relative
influence:

| Factor | Approximate relative influence |
|---|---|
| Sleep | ~60% |
| Training | ~25% |
| Nutrition | ~15% |

Treat this split as a simplification that communicates *ordering*, not a
literal, precisely measured weighting — the underlying point is that without
adequate sleep, neither training nor nutrition can produce their full
effect (sleep affects muscle protein synthesis, growth-hormone and
testosterone release, cortisol regulation, insulin sensitivity, and
hunger-hormone regulation). Practical consequence stated directly in the
source material: **optimize sleep before increasing training volume, and
optimize sleep before pursuing an aggressive diet deficit.**

A commonly cited target is 7–8 hours of consistent sleep, plus circadian
support (morning daylight exposure, a cool bedroom, warm/dim evening light,
consistent sleep/wake times including weekends, and a daily 1–2 hour
wind-down routine).

**Cardiovascular fitness** is described as a related constraint that becomes
more limiting as muscle mass increases (larger muscle needs more oxygen and
nutrient delivery and faster waste clearance to recover between sets).
Suggested approach: keep 80–90% of cardio at low intensity (below lactate
threshold, "conversational" Zone 2), plus general daily movement (roughly
7,000–10,000 steps), on the view that this does not compete with lifting for
recovery and improves nutrient partitioning and work capacity.

**Psychological adherence** is framed as arguably the largest practical
barrier of all: impatience and difficulty delaying gratification are
described as a bigger obstacle to body-composition success than gaps in
training or nutrition knowledge. It shows up as overly aggressive surpluses
or deficits, volume increases that outrun recovery, program-hopping before a
fair trial period, and abandoning a plan at a normal plateau. Counter-
strategies emphasized in the source material: track progress systematically
and judge by trend rather than day-to-day noise, use realistic timeframes
(the "rule of 2" from section 1), and treat process-adherence — sticking to
the strategy — as the primary goal rather than the outcome itself.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Chasing protein past 2.2 g/kg for hypertrophy.** The evidence summarized
  here shows no additional muscle-growth benefit above that range; it is not
  "extra safety margin," just a different macro allocation.
- **Using one calorie target year-round.** The periodisation split above is
  the point — indefinite surplus or indefinite deficit both underperform a
  planned mix of surplus, maintenance, and deficit phases.
- **Jumping straight to "Best" tier tracking permanently.** The three-tier
  system is meant to flex with phase and life circumstances; treating "Best"
  as the only acceptable tier usually collapses adherence.
- **Reading the 60/25/15 split as a precise measurement.** It is presented
  here as an ordering and a simplification, not a literal formula.
- **Increasing training volume or diet aggressiveness before fixing sleep.**
  The stated priority order is sleep first.
- **Treating impatience as a knowledge gap.** More nutrition facts do not
  fix a process where the real failure mode is abandoning a plan too early
  or overreacting to short-term fluctuations.
