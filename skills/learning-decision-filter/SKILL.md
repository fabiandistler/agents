# Learning Decision Filter

Not everything worth knowing is worth learning right now. This skill applies a
five-criterion gate to a single candidate learning investment — a technology, a
course, a certification, a deep-dive topic — and returns a reasoned go/no-go.
Its purpose is narrow and specific: catch **resume-driven development**, the
habit of chasing hype-technologies or trending skills because they look good on
paper, with no real strategic payoff for the person doing the learning.

The filter is deliberately simple: score five criteria, require at least three
to hold, and show the reasoning. It is not a scoring formula to game — a
learning investment that clears the bar on paper but still feels wrong is a
signal to look harder at the criteria, not to override them silently.

## The five gate criteria

Score each criterion as met or not met for the specific learning investment
under consideration. Be honest rather than generous — a criterion only counts
if it holds concretely, not "sort of" or "could eventually."

| # | Criterion | What it asks |
|---|-----------|---------------|
| 1 | **Career driver alignment** | Does this learning serve the career driver(s) that matter most in the person's current life phase (see below)? |
| 2 | **3–5 year relevance** | Is it plausible this skill or knowledge is still relevant in three to five years, rather than a trend likely to fade? |
| 3 | **Competitive advantage** | Is it rare *and* valuable — something that differentiates the person — rather than a commodity skill everyone already has or can pick up trivially? |
| 4 | **Immediate value in the current role** | Would it produce usable value in the person's current job or projects soon, not only in some hypothetical future role? |
| 5 | **Intrinsic enthusiasm** | Is there genuine interest in the topic itself — the sustainability factor that keeps learning going past the initial motivation burst? |

## The gate rule

**At least 3 of 5 criteria must be met for a "go."**

- 4–5 met: strong go — invest the time with confidence.
- 3 met: go, but note which two criteria are weak; watch for those gaps
  turning the effort into a dead end (e.g., high enthusiasm and short-term
  value but no 3–5 year horizon — fine for a quick exploration, risky for a
  multi-month commitment).
- 0–2 met: no-go as currently framed. Name explicitly which criteria fail
  and why — this is usually where resume-driven development is caught: a
  technology that is trending (feels urgent) but fails alignment, durability,
  and rarity all at once.

A "no-go" is not necessarily "never." It usually means: reframe the learning
goal, wait for a life-phase or role shift that changes the weighting, or treat
it as light personal curiosity rather than a strategic investment.

## Criterion 1 depends on career driver alignment by life phase

Criterion 1 cannot be scored without first knowing which career drivers
currently matter most. Career decisions are shaped by five factors:
**Compensation, Culture, Work Content, Growth Opportunities, and Work-Life
Balance.** Their relative priority shifts naturally with life phase:

| Life phase | Drivers that typically dominate |
|---|---|
| Early career | Growth, Work Content |
| Family-formation phase | Work-Life Balance, stability |
| Peak career | Work Content, impact |
| Later career | Legacy, mentorship, sense of purpose |

Before scoring criterion 1, establish (or ask) which phase currently applies
and which one or two drivers are the priority. A learning investment that
would clearly serve "early career, growth-driven" reasoning can score
differently for the same person a few years later once life-phase priorities
have shifted — this is expected, not a contradiction. Re-check this alignment
periodically (e.g., quarterly) rather than assuming it is fixed, since it is
the input most likely to silently go stale between decisions.

## Workflow

1. **Name the candidate learning investment** precisely — a specific
   technology, course, or topic, not a vague area ("get better at AI").
2. **Establish current life phase and dominant career drivers** if not
   already known — ask if unclear. This is the input for criterion 1.
3. **Score each of the five criteria** as met/not met, with one line of
   concrete reasoning each. Resist scoring a criterion "met" on vibes or FOMO
   — require a specific reason.
4. **Apply the ≥3-of-5 rule** to reach go / go-with-caveats / no-go.
5. **Name the weak criteria explicitly**, even on a "go" — they are the risk
   surface for the investment turning out to be a poor use of time.
6. **State the verdict plainly**, using the output format below.

## Output format

```
Candidate: <the specific learning investment>
Life phase / dominant drivers: <phase> — <driver(s)>

1. Career driver alignment      [met/not met] — <why>
2. 3–5 year relevance            [met/not met] — <why>
3. Competitive advantage          [met/not met] — <why>
4. Immediate value in current role [met/not met] — <why>
5. Intrinsic enthusiasm           [met/not met] — <why>

Score: <n>/5
Verdict: <go / go-with-caveats / no-go>
Reasoning: <one to two sentences tying the score to the decision>
```

Keep it proportional — a clear-cut case needs a short version of this, not a
long essay per criterion.

## Common mistakes

- **Scoring enthusiasm as sufficient on its own.** Intrinsic enthusiasm is one
  of five criteria, not a trump card. A person can be genuinely excited about a
  technology that still fails the other four — that is exactly the
  resume-driven-development pattern this filter exists to catch.
- **Skipping the life-phase check.** Criterion 1 is meaningless without first
  naming which drivers currently matter; scoring it from a stale or assumed
  life phase produces a false "go."
- **Treating "trending" as evidence for criterion 2 or 3.** Popularity right
  now says nothing about relevance in 3–5 years, and widespread adoption
  usually works against rarity (criterion 3), not for it.
- **Using the filter to justify a decision already made.** The point is to let
  the score change the decision when the reasoning is honest, not to
  reverse-engineer a "met" on three criteria to greenlight something already
  decided.

## Scope and origin

This filter is one gate within a broader personal career-navigation approach
that also covers T-shaped skill breadth/depth and longer-horizon planning;
those are out of scope here. The criteria and phrasing originate from one
person's personal knowledge base and career-strategy notes — treat them as a
general, reusable decision aid rather than a universal or prescriptive
career model, and adapt the life-phase categories and driver weights to
whoever is actually making the decision.
